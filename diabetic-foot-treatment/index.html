Clinical Staging

Diabetic foot ulcer severity & risk stages

Loss of sensation (peripheral neuropathy) means minor blisters or calluses can develop into deep bone-involving ulcers without causing acute pain.

Peripheral Neuropathy (Numbness)

Tingling, burning sensations (pins and needles), or complete loss of pain, heat, and cold sensation in the toes and soles.

Calluses & Pressure Sores

Thick hardened skin under the metatarsal heads that can break down internally, causing hidden abscesses beneath the callus.

Non-Healing Diabetic Ulcers

Shallow or deep crater-like wounds that discharge serous fluid or pus and fail to form healthy pink granulation tissue over 2 weeks.

Cellulitis & Poor Arterial Flow

Redness spreading up the foot, localized warmth, swelling, black discoloration (gangrene), or absent pedal pulses requiring urgent limb salvage.

Comprehensive Salvage Care

Advanced wound healing & limb salvage protocols

Our multidisciplinary team combines surgical wound debridement with biological tissue scaffolding and offloading biomechanics.

Phase 1

Surgical Debridement

Painless removal of non-viable necrotic slough, biofilm, and bacterial colonization to expose healthy bleeding tissue margins.

Phase 2

Negative Pressure (VAC)

Controlled sub-atmospheric suction removes exudate, reduces bacterial burden, and accelerates rapid capillary granulation tissue growth.

Phase 3

Vascular Revascularization

Evaluation with duplex Doppler and angioplasty when peripheral artery disease (PAD) restricts oxygen delivery to the healing wound.

Phase 4

Custom Offloading Orthotics

Total contact casting, therapeutic footwear, and custom silicone orthotics redistribute weight away from vulnerable ulcer beds.

Clinical Insights

Frequently asked questions about diabetic foot care

Understanding early warning signs and proper wound protocols can save limbs and safeguard independent mobility.

Why do diabetic foot ulcers take so long to heal?

Elevated blood sugar levels impair white blood cell function, decrease arterial blood flow (microangiopathy), and cause nerve damage (neuropathy). Without adequate oxygen and normal sensations to rest the foot, wounds cannot close spontaneously and require active clinical wound debridement.

Why does my diabetic foot ulcer not hurt?

This is the most dangerous aspect of diabetic peripheral neuropathy. Nerve damage numbs pain receptors, so patients continue walking on an open sore without feeling discomfort, driving bacteria and pressure deeper into the foot structures.

Can amputation really be avoided?

Yes. Studies demonstrate that early specialized intervention, aggressive debridement of infected tissue, negative pressure vacuum therapy, targeted antibiotics, and pressure offloading successfully prevent major amputations in over 85% of cases.

What daily routine should people with diabetes follow?

Inspect the bottom of both feet daily using a mirror for redness, cracks, or cuts. Wash with lukewarm water, dry thoroughly between the toes, moisturize the heels, avoid walking barefoot even at home, and wear seam-free diabetic socks.
